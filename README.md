# NoiseAudit
## Noise audit of aviation forecasts

Terminal Aerodrome Forecasts must be accurate, consistent, and operationally interpretable across forecasters performing the same task. Conventional verification measures forecast skill and bias, but rarely examines forecast noise: unwanted variability in judgement between forecasters facing comparable meteorological situations. This study adapts Kahneman’s noise-audit framework to operational aviation forecasting using matched forecast and observation archives from major Australian airports during 2025. The analysis examines forecast variability across three complementary dimensions: hazard assessment, quantitative forecast accuracy, and communication of uncertainty and forecast evolution. Rather than treating forecast performance as a single measure of skill, the paper develops a multidimensional framework that distinguishes systematic bias from differences in judgement, technical forecast performance, and communication practice. The results show that operational forecast variability is not primarily a problem of directional bias. Instead, inconsistency arises from differences in how forecasters assess marginal hazards, interpret uncertain or evolving conditions, and express that uncertainty to aviation users. Communication choices emerge as a particularly important source of variability, indicating that forecasters may hold broadly similar meteorological expectations while constructing materially different forecast products. The study argues that forecast consistency should be monitored alongside traditional verification metrics and that improvements may come from targeted calibration, structured decision-making, peer review, and clearer standards for communicating uncertainty. By making forecast noise measurable, the framework provides a practical basis for improving the reliability and interchangeability of operational aviation forecasts.

# Significance:
Operational aviation forecasting has traditionally focused on reducing forecast bias and improving forecast skill. However, forecasters can produce substantially different forecasts even when presented with similar meteorological information. This variability, known as forecast noise, is rarely measured despite its potential operational consequences for pilots, air traffic managers, and aviation decision-makers.
This study introduces a practical noise-audit framework for Terminal Aerodrome Forecasts (TAFs) that quantifies variability in hazard assessment, forecast accuracy, and forecast communication. Using operational forecasts from multiple Australian airports, the analysis shows that forecast bias is comparatively small, while significant variability exists between forecasters. Communication noise was found to exceed both hazard-judgement noise and forecast-accuracy noise, indicating that differences in how uncertainty and forecast evolution are communicated may contribute as much to forecast variability as differences in meteorological skill itself.

The results suggest that improvements in forecast consistency may be achieved not only through better science and prediction systems, but also through structured decision-making, forecast calibration, communication standardization, and targeted training for high-noise weather situations. This work provides a framework for routinely monitoring forecast noise alongside traditional verification metrics and offers a new approach for improving the reliability and interchangeability of operational aviation forecasts.
